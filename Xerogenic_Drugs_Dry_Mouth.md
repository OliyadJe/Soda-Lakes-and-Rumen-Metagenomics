# Xerogenic Drugs (Medications That Cause Dry Mouth / Xerostomia)

A deep-research, evidence-based reference table of drugs and drug classes that cause
**xerostomia** (the subjective sensation of dry mouth) and/or **salivary gland
hypofunction** (objectively reduced salivary flow).

> **Scope & method.** Compiled from a multi-source, adversarially fact-checked
> literature review. The backbone is the *World Workshop on Oral Medicine VI*
> (WWOM VI) PRISMA systematic review, the definitive evidence-graded catalogue of
> medications affecting salivary function, supplemented by a quantitative
> meta-analysis (odds ratios) and recent primary studies. Every row links to the
> reference numbers in the [References](#references) section. Claims that failed
> verification are listed under [What the evidence does *not* support](#what-the-evidence-does-not-support).
>
> ⚠️ **This document is for educational/research use only. It is not medical advice.**
> Do not start, stop, or change any medication based on this table — consult a
> qualified clinician or pharmacist.

## How to read the "Strength/Frequency" column

- **Very high** – among the highest-risk, most-cited xerogenic agents; frequently
  causes moderate-to-severe dry mouth (e.g., bladder antimuscarinics, TCAs).
- **High** – consistently and commonly associated with dry mouth across studies.
- **Moderate** – well-documented but generally milder or less frequent.
- **Low/Indirect** – dry mouth occurs via secondary mechanisms (e.g., fluid
  depletion, mucosal drying) rather than direct muscarinic blockade.

Where available, quantitative anchors are given: pooled odds ratios (OR) from the
Tan et al. meta-analysis [2], or measured salivary-flow reductions.

## Core mechanism (one-paragraph summary)

Most drug-induced dry mouth is driven by **anticholinergic (antimuscarinic)
activity**: the drug blocks acetylcholine from binding **muscarinic M1/M3
receptors (M3-predominant)** on salivary acinar cells, preventing the
parasympathetic (cholinergic) signal that drives salivary fluid secretion [1][5][7].
A drug's xerogenic strength tracks its antimuscarinic binding potency — a lower
inhibition constant (Ki) means tighter receptor blockade and more dry mouth (e.g.,
amitriptyline Ki ≈ 25.9 vs. trazodone Ki ≈ 324,000, a ~12,500-fold difference) [5].
Additional, non-muscarinic routes also contribute: **α1-adrenergic blockade**,
**central serotonergic/sympathomimetic effects**, and **fluid/volume depletion**
(diuretics). A useful mechanistic "negative control": the β3-agonist **mirabegron**,
which has *no* antimuscarinic activity, causes only placebo-level dry mouth
(~1.6% vs. ~2.1% placebo) despite treating the same condition (overactive bladder)
as the high-risk antimuscarinics [6].

## Quantitative anchors (the strongest numbers)

| Finding | Value | Source |
|---------|-------|--------|
| Bladder antimuscarinics (urologicals) — risk of dry mouth | **OR 5.91** (95% CI 4.04–8.63) | Tan et al. meta-analysis [2] |
| Antidepressants — risk of dry mouth | **OR 4.74** (95% CI 2.69–8.32) | Tan et al. meta-analysis [2] |
| Psycholeptics (antipsychotics/sedatives) — risk | **OR 2.59** (95% CI 1.79–3.95) | Tan et al. meta-analysis [2] |
| Anticholinergic drugs overall — dry mouth frequency | **16–30%** (most common anticholinergic adverse effect) | [5] |
| Solifenacin dose-response (unstimulated salivary flow) | 5 mg **−16.6%**, 10 mg **−19.3%** (both p<0.001) | Scientific Reports 2025 [6] |
| Mirabegron (β3-agonist, *no* antimuscarinic activity) | dry mouth **~1.6%** vs placebo ~2.1% (negative control) | Scientific Reports 2025 [6] |
| Drugs catalogued by WWOM VI with evidence of causing salivary dysfunction | **56 strong + 50 moderate** evidence; **64** ATC drug classes (37 stronger evidence) | Wolff et al. [1] |
| Share of strong-evidence xerogenic drugs acting on the nervous system | **>50%** (36 of 56) | Wolff et al. [1] |

**The 10 most-cited xerogenic drugs in the literature** (Wolff et al. WWOM VI [1]):
oxybutynin, tolterodine, duloxetine, quetiapine, bupropion, olanzapine,
solifenacin, clozapine, fluoxetine, venlafaxine — i.e., **bladder antimuscarinics
and psychotropics dominate**.

## Key takeaways

1. **Anticholinergic activity is the dominant driver.** Hundreds of medications
   (commonly cited as >600) possess anticholinergic properties, and dry mouth is
   their single most common adverse effect (16–30%) [5].
2. **Two classes carry the highest risk:** urological **bladder antimuscarinics**
   (OR ≈ 5.91) and **antidepressants**, especially **TCAs** (OR ≈ 4.74) [2].
3. **Potency predicts severity** — xerogenic strength tracks muscarinic binding
   affinity (Ki) and is **dose-dependent** [5][6].
4. **Polypharmacy / cumulative anticholinergic burden** compounds the effect;
   the number of medications taken is itself associated with xerostomia [1][2][9].
5. **Not all drugs for the same indication are equal** — mirabegron causes
   placebo-level dry mouth, confirming the muscarinic basis of the effect [6].

## What the evidence does *not* support

During adversarial verification, the following claims **failed** fact-checking and
should **not** be cited as reliable:

- ❌ Specific prevalence percentages from one narrative review — "amitriptyline
  30–50%, paroxetine 20–40%, clozapine 10–30%, anticholinergics 20–65%."
  These were **refuted** (came from a non-systematic narrative review and were not
  corroborated). Use the meta-analytic **odds ratios** [2] for relative risk
  instead of these point prevalences.
- ⚠️ The ">600 anticholinergic medications" figure has no firm inline citation and
  exceeds the classic ">400" figure; treat as order-of-magnitude.
- ⚠️ For SSRIs/SNRIs/antipsychotics, framing **all** psychotropic dry mouth as
  purely muscarinic is a simplification — serotonergic and α1-adrenergic pathways
  also contribute.
- ⚠️ Adverse-event database counts (e.g., Australian ADAEN: 1,927 dry-mouth case
  reports, 1971–2020 [8]) reflect **reporting patterns, not true incidence**.
